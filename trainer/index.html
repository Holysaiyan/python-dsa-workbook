Number tips: every operation

Before every level

  1. Read the operation first. Add, difference, multiply and divide each need a different method below. Picking with the wrong operation in mind is the easiest point to lose.
  2. Check one number at a time. Never try pairs at random. Take one number, work out the partner it needs, and look for that partner.
  3. Check before the last click. Say the sum or product in your head. Half a second here saves a wrong pick, which costs more than a slow one.

Add to

  1. Target minus one number. Add to 31 with 17, 13, 19, 12: 17 needs 14 (not there); 19 needs 12 (there). Answer 19 + 12.
  2. Last digit check. For 31 the last digits must make a number ending in 1: 9 + 2, 7 + 4, 3 + 8, 6 + 5, 0 + 1. 17 + 13 ends in 0, so it is wrong before you add the tens.
  3. Odd or even. An odd target needs one odd and one even number. An even target needs two odds or two evens.
  4. Three numbers become two. Take the biggest, subtract it from the target, then find a pair for what is left. No pair? Put it back and start with the next biggest.

Have a difference of (subtract)

  1. Add the target to each small number. Difference 17 with 45, 28, 32, 16, 19: 16 + 17 = 33 (no), 19 + 17 = 36 (no), 28 + 17 = 45 (yes). Answer 45 and 28.
  2. Start with the smallest numbers. The smaller number of the pair is always the starting point, so work up from the bottom.
  3. The top number is always bigger than the target. With a difference of 30, nothing of 30 or under can be the top of the pair; those numbers can only be the bottom.
  4. Last digit check. 45 and 28: going from 8 up to 15 is 7, matching the 7 in 17.
  5. Odd or even. An odd difference needs one odd and one even number. An even difference needs two odds or two evens.

Multiply to

  1. Think in factor pairs of the target. For 72 ask "what times what?": 8 × 9, 6 × 12. Then look for both numbers. Do not multiply pairs from the board one by one.
  2. Odd target: both numbers are odd, so every even number is out.
  3. Even target: at least one number is even.
  4. Ends in 0 or 5: one number is 5, 10 or another multiple of 5.
  5. Size check. Two numbers under 10 cannot make more than 81. If the target is bigger, one number is 10 or more.
  6. Learn the slippery facts. These are the ones most often confused:
    6 × 7 = 426 × 8 = 486 × 9 = 547 × 7 = 497 × 8 = 567 × 9 = 638 × 8 = 648 × 9 = 729 × 9 = 8111 × 12 = 13212 × 6 = 7212 × 7 = 8412 × 8 = 9612 × 9 = 10812 × 12 = 144

Divide to give

  1. Turn it into multiplying. Small number × target = big number. Divide to give 7 with 56, 9, 8, 30, 4: 4 × 7 = 28 (no), 8 × 7 = 56 (yes). Answer 56 ÷ 8.
  2. Go through the small numbers. Multiply each by the target and look for the result on the board.
  3. Odd big number: it cannot be divided exactly by an even number, so pair it only with odd numbers.
  4. Divisibility shortcuts. Divisible by 3 or 9: the digits add up to a multiple of 3 or 9 (54: 5 + 4 = 9). By 5: ends in 0 or 5. By 4: the last two digits divide by 4 (56: yes). By 6: even and divisible by 3.

Level 10:15
Which option is the same shape, turned?
How to playThe right option is the target turned round (90, 180 or 270 degrees, any direction). The others are a mirror image or have one square moved. On Ultimate, more than one option can be right: tick every match, then Submit. Keys 1 to 6 pick, Enter submits.
Watch: Shapes explainer, 2 min
Shape tips
  1. Turned, never flipped. A shape can be turned to any of four positions, but no amount of turning makes its mirror image. A mirror option is always wrong, and it is the trap most people fall into because it looks right at a glance.
  2. Follow one landmark. Pick a distinctive square in the target, such as a corner or the end of a line. Turned a quarter clockwise, the top row becomes the right-hand column, the right-hand column becomes the bottom row, and so on round.
  3. Check a second square. One square in the right place proves nothing: mirror images share many squares. Confirm a second landmark before you pick.
  4. Count first. Every correct option has the same number of filled squares as the target. A "one square moved" option has the right count but the wrong layout, so count, then check the layout.
  5. Rule out, then choose. If one option is obviously the same shape flipped, cross it out mentally and compare the rest.
  6. Do not over-check. Once two landmarks match, pick. Time is part of the score.
  7. Any direction. The answer can be turned clockwise, anticlockwise or upside down. Do not guess the direction: find where your landmark corner went, and that tells you the turn. Corners only ever go to corners.
  8. Big grids: pick the loneliest square. On 5 by 5 grids, choose a square with the fewest neighbours, or the end of the longest line. It is easier to spot after a turn than a square in a clump.
  9. Moved square tricks. On harder levels a square is moved by only one step. If two landmarks match but something feels off, check the square furthest from your landmark.
  10. More than one right answer. Some versions ask you to pick every match. Check each option on its own merits; do not stop at the first match, and do not assume there must be two.

Level 10:10
Tap the one that is different
How to playAll shapes match except one: it is turned, a different shape, or a slightly different size. Scan row by row instead of jumping around. Speed counts, but a wrong tap costs more than a slow one.
Watch: Odd one out explainer, 1 min
Odd one out tips
  1. Scan in a fixed order. Row by row, left to right, like reading. Jumping around makes you look at the same shapes twice.
  2. Use your side vision. On small grids, look at the centre of the grid and let the odd shape pop out. Only scan row by row if nothing stands out in a second.
  3. Check one feature at a time. First the direction a point or arrow faces, then the size, then the outline. The difference is always one of these.
  4. Size differences are small. On harder levels the odd one may be only slightly smaller. Compare it with its neighbours, not with a shape across the grid.
  5. Accuracy beats speed. A wrong tap usually costs more than an extra second.

How to solve each level (short videos)
Length 4Ready
Watch the digits, then type them in order 
How to playDigits appear one at a time. Group them in your head in threes, like a phone number: 4 7 2, 9 1 5. Say them silently as they appear. Type with the keypad or your keyboard. Two right in a row adds a digit; a miss takes one away.
Watch: Memory explainer, 1 min
Memory tips
  1. Chunk in threes. 4 7 2 9 1 5 is six things to hold; 472, 915 is two. It is how phone numbers are remembered.
  2. Say them silently. Repeat the chunk in your head as each digit appears. Hearing it uses a different memory from seeing it, and both together hold more.
  3. Spot patterns. Runs (3 4 5), repeats (7 7) and familiar numbers (a year, a door number) are free memory.
  4. Do not look away. Keep your eyes on where the digits appear until the last one has gone.
  5. Type straight away. Recall fades in seconds. Type the first chunk the moment your turn starts.
  6. Letters too. Some memory games mix letters and digits. Chunk them the same way and say them as a sound: K 4 P is "kay-four-pee".

Warm-up12 choices
Which is more like you at work?
What this isCandidates report BAE's personality game shows pictures and asks which is more like you. It measures work style, not ability, and checks you answer consistently. This warm-up uses words instead of pictures so you get used to the pace: about 5 seconds a choice, first honest reaction. There is no right answer. Keys 1 and 2 pick.

BAE's values

BAE Systems' values (from BAE's own careers site). BAE says these are what it looks for, so know them, but answer as you really are at work:

Trusted
  • Being committed: giving our best every day
  • Doing what we say we will
  • Doing the right thing

Your evidence: compliance work in a regulated sector, where accuracy, procedure and owning your mistakes matter.

Innovative
  • Being curious: seeking new ways to make things better
  • Learning constantly, from successes and failures
  • Turning ideas into actions

Your evidence: moving from compliance into software, and finding a new route for your meal planner project (Story 1).

Bold
  • Being courageous: speaking up, stepping forward
  • Seizing opportunities
  • Realising big ambitions

Your evidence: raising your situation with your supervisor in third year and agreeing a new plan, instead of quietly struggling on.

  1. Use the values as a lens, not a script. When two images both feel true, pick the one closest to how you behave on a good day at work: owning things, staying curious, speaking up.
  2. Do not try to game it. Guessing a "perfect" profile makes answers contradict each other, and consistency is what these games check.
  3. First honest reaction, a few seconds each. Think of yourself at work, not with friends.
  4. Reuse them in your video answers. Name the value your story shows in your last sentence: "That is what doing the right thing means to me."

On the day: all games
  1. Use every practice round. Each real game gives you a practice round first. Use it to learn the controls and the rule, not to rush.
  2. The games get harder when you do well. Feeling stretched means you are doing well, not badly. Do not let one hard level shake you.
  3. One wrong answer is one point. Move on straight away; dwelling on it costs the next level too.
  4. Use a mouse or trackpad you are used to, on a laptop or desktop rather than a phone, in a quiet room, with notifications off.
  5. Water and a short break between the video questions and the games.

These tips cover the games candidates describe for BAE Systems and the common HireVue types. The real games are not published, so expect some surprises; the practice round is there for exactly that.
